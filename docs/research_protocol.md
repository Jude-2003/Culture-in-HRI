## 1. Introduction
 
### 1.1 Background
 
Robots are increasingly deployed in social and service settings globally, yet the majority of Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) research has been conducted with Western participants, leaving a significant gap in understanding how people from different cultural backgrounds respond to robot behaviour. Culture shapes the norms and expectations people bring to social encounters, and there is growing evidence that this extends to interactions with robots.
 
Cross-cultural HRI studies have consistently found that cultural background influences robot attitudes and preferences. Bartneck et al. (2007) demonstrated significant differences in negative attitudes toward robots between Japanese and Western participants. Wang et al. (2010) found that American participants preferred robots using explicit, direct communication while Chinese participants preferred implicit styles. Salem et al. (2014) showed that Arabic participants responded more positively to polite robot language than Western participants. More recently, Korn et al. (2021) found meaningful differences in robot preferences between German and Arab participants, including Saudi Arabians, across multiple social contexts.
 
Despite this accumulating evidence, most cross-cultural HRI studies identify cultural differences after the fact and attribute them retrospectively to broad cultural narratives, without deriving specific predictions from validated cultural frameworks in advance. Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions framework provides a validated instrument for quantifying national cultural differences and has been applied successfully in technology acceptance research. Srite and Karahanna (2006) demonstrated that Uncertainty Avoidance and Individualism significantly moderated technology adoption intentions using individual-level Hofstede measures, providing direct precedent for the present study's approach. However, this methodology has rarely been applied in controlled experimental HRI paradigms, and Power Distance and Uncertainty Avoidance, two dimensions with direct theoretical relevance to robot interaction, remain underexplored. The present study addresses this gap by deriving and testing specific, pre-registered hypotheses for both dimensions in a controlled laboratory setting, using a physical robot and interactive game-based tasks.
 
### 1.2 Study Rationale
 
Over the past decade, social robots (machines designed to interact with people in natural, communicative ways) have begun appearing in hospitals, hotels, schools, and public spaces across the world. Unlike industrial robots, which operate in controlled environments away from people, social robots are designed to share space with humans, answer questions, provide assistance, and engage in conversation. Their deployment is accelerating: the global service robotics market is growing rapidly, driven by labour shortages, ageing populations, and the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, which normalized contactless service delivery across many sectors.
 
A robot designed to be friendly and collaborative, adopting a peer-like tone and adapting its responses to the user, may feel natural and appropriate to someone from the United Kingdom. The same robot, deployed in Riyadh, may feel presumptuous, unpredictable, or simply wrong, not because the technology has failed, but because the interaction style was never designed with that cultural context in mind. This is the central problem the present project addresses. Ultimately, this project aims to contribute to the development of culturally adaptive social robots: systems capable of operating effectively and appropriately across diverse social environments, rather than assuming a single interaction style transfers universally.
 
There are two pre-registered directional hypotheses.
 
**H1:** Controlling for nationally patterned contextual factors, higher individual-level PDI scores will predict more positive attitudinal responses, on measures of perceived appropriateness and trust, treated as indicators of a common evaluative construct, to a robot displaying directive, authoritative communication behaviours, and more negative responses to peer-like, collaborative robot behaviour. At the national group level, Saudi Arabian participants are expected to exhibit the same directional pattern relative to UK participants, to the extent that national group differences reflect underlying PDI orientation.
 
**H2:** Higher individual-level UAI scores will predict more positive attitudinal responses, on measure of perceived appropriateness and trust, treated as indicators of a common evaluative construct, to a robot displaying predictable, rule-governed communication behaviour, relative to adaptive, open-ended behaviour. At the national group level, Saudi Arabian participants are expected to show the same directional pattern relative to UK participants. This prediction is conditional on participants experiencing the adaptive robot condition as uncertain rather than merely flexible, and effects are interpreted as consistent with UAI theory rather than uniquely attributed to it.

## 2. Study Objectives
 
The overarching aim of this project is to determine whether Power Distance and Uncertainty Avoidance, measured both at the national level and at the level of individual cultural orientation, systematically predict how people respond to specific robot communication styles, and to use those findings to inform thinking about culturally adaptive robot design.
 
A key methodological contribution is the project's treatment of culture not merely as a national label but as an individual-level variable. Previous cross-cultural HRI research has typically compared national groups and assumed that any observed differences reflect cultural values; but this conflates cultural orientation with many other nationally patterned factors, such as prior technology exposure, economic context, and religion. This study incorporates selected items from the Values Survey Module 2013 (VSM-2013), a validated instrument derived from Hofstede's framework, to assess work-related values and attitudes relevant to power distance and uncertainty avoidance. These items are used as individual-level measures of participants' attitudes rather than as individual cultural dimension scores. The items will be analysed individually or combined into theoretically justified scales where appropriate, alongside national group membership. This approach allows the study to examine whether individual attitudes and values provide explanatory power beyond national group membership without attributing national-level VSM cultural-dimension scores to individual participants.
 
The project also incorporates a qualitative component: open-response questions administered alongside quantitative measures and analysed thematically to illuminate the interpretive frameworks participants draw on when evaluating robot behaviour. This mixed-methods design provides explanatory depth that quantitative measures alone cannot offer.
 
The specific objectives are to conduct a critical literature review of cross-cultural HRI and cultural psychology; develop and validate an experimental design with appropriate stimuli and measures; collect and analyse data from UK and Saudi Arabian participants; test two pre-registered directional hypotheses; and produce a final report with findings suitable for submission to an academic venue.

## 3. Study Design
 
The empirical component of this project comprises two parallel experimental studies, one for each cultural dimension under investigation. Each study follows a between-subjects design in which participants are randomly assigned to view one of two robot behaviour conditions, differing only on the target dimension, and interact with a robot-guided game delivered via a Unity-based interface.
 
The study employs a physically present robot (Reachy Mini) as the primary interaction medium. Prior to the lab game, participants will complete a pre-study questionnaire through Qualtrics which will collect data on participant demographics, prior experience with robots, and personal values. Participants then sit in a laboratory facing a laptop with the game interface displayed on it, and the robot, which speaks and moves, sitting next to them while guiding them through the game. The game is conducted via Unity and the robot's behaviour will be controlled in real time with dialogue and movement triggered at appropriate moments in the interaction. Once the game is completed, participants will complete a post-study Qualtrics survey which will collect data on general attitudes towards robots, impressions on the robot in the study, trust in the robot, and general thoughts on the robot.
 
Data that will be collected are names, emails, and WhatsApp numbers at registration, survey responses through Qualtrics, and in-task decisions through Unity. The robot itself will not collect any data and will not record audio or video.

## 4. Participant Recruitment
 
### 4.1 Pre-recruitment Evaluations
 
Participants will complete a brief demographic pre-screen before their session to confirm eligibility, covering nationality and country of upbringing. This pre-screen is not an evaluative test but a straightforward eligibility check, as the study only involves participants from the United Kingdom and Saudi Arabia.
 
### 4.2 Inclusion Criteria
 
Participants must:
 
- Be aged 18 or over
- Be university students
- Fluent in English
- Be from the United Kingdom or Saudi Arabia and have spent the majority of their life before age 18 in one of these two countries

**Justification:** The study specifically requires participants from the UK and Saudi Arabia because these two countries exhibit one of the largest contrasting profiles on Power Distance and Uncertainty Avoidance of any country pairing globally, maximising the study's sensitivity to detecting hypothesised cultural effects. University recruitment provides direct access to both national groups within a single institution. English fluency is required because the robot interaction and questionnaire are administered in English.
 
### 4.3 Exclusion Criteria
 
- Under 18 years of age: to ensure all participants can provide informed adult consent
- Non-fluent in English: the robot interaction and all study materials are in English; insufficient fluency would compromise comprehension and data quality
- Not from and has not spent the majority of their life before age 18 in either the UK or Saudi Arabia: to ensure participants' cultural orientations reflect the national profiles targeted by the study design; long-term expatriates or individuals with highly mixed national backgrounds may not reflect the cultural orientation of either target group
### 4.4 Withdrawal Criteria
 
Participants will be told that they can withdraw at any time of the project by informing the researcher. There are no consequences for the participants of withdrawing from the study. After withdrawal all data collected from the participant will be deleted, including the linkage file to their pseudonym.

## 6. Statistics and Data Analysis
 
### 6.1 Sample Size
 
The target sample size is 10 participants across both studies (n = 5 participants per study). Within each study, participants will be drawn from the two national groups and assigned to the two experimental conditions. This sample size was selected based on the feasibility and time constraints of the study.
 
**Primary outcome:** Perceived appropriateness of robot communication style and trust, measured on validated Likert scales (Godspeed, Trust in Automation).
 
**Assumed effect size:** A medium effect size of f = 0.25 is assumed, consistent with effect sizes reported in comparable cross-cultural technology acceptance studies (Srite & Karahanna, 2006) and cross-cultural HRI studies (Bartneck et al., 2007). This effect size is used to inform interpretation rather than to claim that the present sample is adequately powered to detect a medium effect.
 
**Statistical power and significance level:** A power of 0.80 is targeted, consistent with standard practice in experimental psychology. The significance level is α = 0.05, two-tailed. These numbers are used as conventional benchmarks.
 
**Calculation:** A power analysis for a two-way ANOVA with two groups and two conditions, assuming f = 0.25, α = 0.05, and power = 0.80, requires substantially more participants than the present feasible sample. Therefore, the current study is considered an exploratory study, and the sample of 10 participants per study is not expected to provide 0.80 power to detect a medium effect. Bayes factors (BF10) will be reported alongside frequentist p-values to enable interpretation of both positive and null findings, and sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the minimum detectable effect at the achieved sample size.
 
### 6.2 Data Analysis
 
Data will be analysed by the principal investigator and will consist of the analyses below. Given the small sample size, all statistical analyses will be treated as exploratory and interpreted primarily through effect sizes, confidence intervals, and Bayes factors rather than statistical significance alone.
 
**Primary outcome analysis:**
The primary outcomes are perceived appropriateness of robot communication style and trust, operationalised through composite scores derived from the Godspeed scales and Trust in Automation scale respectively. For each dimension study, exploratory two-way ANOVA will examine the effects of robot condition (directive vs. peer-like for PDI; predictable vs. adaptive for UAI) and national group (UK vs. Saudi Arabia), and their interaction. Planned contrasts will be used to examine the directional hypotheses specified in H1 and H2. Effect sizes will be reported as partial eta-squared and Bayes factors (BF10) will accompany all primary tests to provide additional evidence regarding the presence or absence of effects. Given the achieved sample size, p-values will not be interpreted as evidence of definitive hypothesis confirmation or rejection.
 
**Regression analysis:**
Selected VSM-2013 items will be analysed as individual-level measures of participants' work-related attitudes and values relevant to power distance and uncertainty avoidance. These individual-level measures will be entered as continuous predictors of the primary outcome variables, alongside national group membership, to examine whether individual attitudes and values provide explanatory power beyond national group membership. Items may be analysed individually or combined into theoretically justified scales where appropriate. Given the small sample size, these regression analyses will be exploratory, with emphasis placed on the direction and magnitude of regression coefficients and effect estimates rather than statistical significance alone.
 
**Qualitative analysis:**
Open-response data will be analysed thematically.
 
**Subgroup analysis:**
Prior robot experience will be recorded and summarised descriptively. Where relevant, prior robot experience will be considered when interpreting individual-level variation in the quantitative and qualitative findings.
 
**Sensitivity analysis:**
Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the minimum detectable effect associated with the achieved sample size and to determine how robust the observed findings are to alternative analytical assumptions. The results will be interpreted as preliminary evidence that could inform future studies with larger, adequately powered samples.

## 7. Regulatory Issues
 
### 7.1 Ethics Approval
 
The Principal Investigator has obtained approval from the Head of Department and favourable opinion from the Science, Engineering and Technology Research Ethics Committee (SETREC).
 
### 7.2 Consent
 
Consent to enter the study must be sought from each participant only after a full explanation has been given, and time allowed for consideration. Signed participant consent should be obtained. The right of the participant to refuse to participate without giving reasons must be respected. All participants are free to withdraw at any time.
 
### 7.3 Confidentiality
 
The Principal Investigator will preserve the confidentiality of participants taking part in the study and fulfil transparency requirements under the General Data Protection Regulation for health and care research. Data and all appropriate documentation will be stored for a minimum of 10 years after the completion of the study, including the follow-up period.
